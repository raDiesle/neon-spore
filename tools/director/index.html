Every creature the design has a name for and the simulation does not: the first thirteen, everything argued into the spec since, and the creature ideas that are accepted but not worked out. The frame beside a name holds the contour drawn at that idea, and the line under the paragraph says why it is that shape. A frame with a question mark in it is a name nobody has drawn anything at yet — SHAPES has the spare contours, and handing one over is a decision rather than an edit here.

Every contour the project has, in three states. A DRAFT was drawn for a named idea in the store and offered to it. A FREE shape has nothing behind it — tuned in legacy/style-guide.html and never spent. TAKEN is what the game already draws, here so a proposal is judged beside what it will stand next to.

They animate, because whether a lobe swings or only shivers is most of what tells two blobs apart at 26 px. The sway is the same data the game draws with, measured in tiles, so a card shows the motion the field would show and not an impression of it.

A shape is not a name, and a suggestion is not a decision. Anything that takes one still gets its own name out of the bestiary's four naming rules, and the idea it was drawn for is free to refuse it.

The skin is the same question docs/alive.md sends to a vote: whether an interior survives at all, or whether the outline is the whole of what a player reads. LINE is the control — it is what these cards drew before there was a choice.

One body, drawn once per skin, once per motion and once per light state, all of them on screen at once — twenty skins flipped one at a time is not a comparison, it is a tournament nobody runs to the end. BULB QUEEN's flanking torches were drawn with nothing holding them — a bare rock in the flank column, and the picture never said why it stayed there. Three drafts answered that, spread on one axis: how much of the holder is machine and how much is her. CRADLE won — her own body doing the holding, and nothing else — and the other two stay below as the record of what it was chosen against. None of this is wired into the game; the shipped queen is unchanged. All three run on one clock, holding for three beats and letting go on the fourth, because how each one lets go is half the question.

THREE WHOLE-BODY DRAFTS, CRADLE AT BOTH FLANKS

Keeping the holder question settled, these three answer a second one: how much of her condition the body itself admits, before the petal row or the sink confirm it. HAIRLINE says all of it, permanently, in the shell. UNDERGLOW says it as light without touching the outline. WITHDRAWAL says it as posture alone. Every card keeps the same two marks with only one real, the same columns either side of her, the same torch-is-the-rock sockets and the same regrowing replacement — only her outline, her material and how she carries a hit are open. All six cards above and below share one clock.

Read off two other co-op games — Hazelight's It Takes Two and Split Fiction — and mapped, row by row, onto whether it can reach this one. Most of it cannot, and the reason is always the same: their unit of play is a character crossing a space and ours is a column. The verdict column is the point, and a NO with its reason in it is worth as much here as a yes.
