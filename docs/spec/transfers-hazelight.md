# What to take from It Takes Two and Split Fiction

> **Status: none of it built, and none of it accepted.** A reading of two more
> reference games against this one's constraints. An entry leaves this page
> either into [the idea store](ideas.md) as a bullet somebody can work out, or
> into the refusals at the bottom with the reason it did not survive the trip.

[transfers](transfers.md) read Spaceteam and Lovers in a Dangerous Spacetime,
which are about **two people operating one machine**. This game is one of those
too, and most of what came back was already here under another name.

Hazelight's two games — It Takes Two (2021) and its successor Split Fiction
(2025) — are a reference for a different thing, and it is the thing this design
has no answer to at all. They are not about one machine. They are about
**never operating the same machine twice**. A level hands the pair a pair of
verbs, spends them for twenty minutes, and throws them away; the next level
hands them two others. Nothing accumulates. There is no upgrade tree, no
growing arsenal, no difficulty curve in the ordinary sense. The engine of both
games is *novelty*, and the unit of novelty is a short self-contained round
with its own rules, its own controls and its own picture.

That is the transfer. Not a creature, not a boss, not a coupling: a **shape of
round** this game does not have.

## Why it is worth reading at all, given the couch

Everything Hazelight does is on one screen, in split-screen, with both players
seeing both halves. That is the opposite of this game's premise, and it means
**not one of their minigames transfers as designed** — every one of them has to
be re-derived for two devices that show different things.

That re-derivation is where the value is. A minigame both players can see all
of is a minigame either of them could learn to play alone while the other
watches. Cut it in half across two devices and the same minigame becomes a
conversation, or it becomes nothing. Which of the two it becomes is the only
question this page asks of each mechanism.

## The filter

The five tests in [transfers](transfers.md#the-filter) all still apply — the
voice delay, nothing travels, 26 px in portrait, it asks the pair something new,
and it never listens. An interlude adds two of its own, and they are strict:

6. **Neither player can play it alone.** A round where one person could do the
   whole thing with the other watching is a minigame, not a Neon Spore
   interlude. The split has to be structural: each player holds either half the
   information or half the verbs, and the round does not resolve without both.
7. **It is learnt in one sentence and over in a minute.** An interlude that
   needs teaching is a system, and systems belong in [systems](systems.md).
   The whole reason to leave the field is that the pair gets a new thing to be
   bad at together for ninety seconds; a new thing to be bad at for an act is a
   different design.

And underneath both, the rule this page cannot decide on its own: **nothing the
players control travels the field** (`CLAUDE.md`, and
[roles](roles.md#there-is-no-ship-movement)). See
[interludes](interludes.md#the-question-the-whole-page-hangs-on) — every idea
below is written on the assumption that the rule is about the *hull*, and that
assumption needs a decision in `docs/decisions.md` before any of them is built.

## It Takes Two — what it actually does

| Mechanism | Here already as | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A pair of asymmetric verbs per level, discarded at its end | nothing — the roles are fixed for the whole run | new, and it is the whole page |
| Optional competitive minigames scattered through the world | nothing | refused as competition, kept as **the interlude** |
| Genre swaps as set pieces: top-down, side-scroller, on-rails, fighting game | nothing | new — see **THE WELL** and **THE EDGE** |
| Split-screen that changes shape mid-scene | two devices; the split is permanent | refused, and recovered — see below |
| Boss fights that need the two verbs in a fixed order | [warding](couplings.md), the whole design | built, in its own form |
| One player pilots while the other aims | the pilot's column and the navigator's trigger | built, and it is the game |
| Time rewind held by one player, a clone placed by the other | nothing; `replay.ts` is the machinery | new — see **THE REPRISE** |

**The competitive minigames are a device, and it is not the obvious one.** A
game that forces cooperation for twelve hours puts twenty-five optional
*competitions* in the scenery — tug of war, whack-a-mole, chess — and they are
not a lapse. They are a release valve: a place to stop needing each other for
thirty seconds. This game has the same problem and no such valve. It also has a
reason not to take this answer, and the reason wins: two devices means a
competition has no shared screen to be decided on, and the pair's only channel
is the one they would have to use to trust each other's score. **What survives
is the pause, not the contest** — see **The Fork** in
[transfers](transfers.md#the-fork), which is already in the idea store and is
the same beat of the run.

## Split Fiction — what it actually does

| Mechanism | Here already as | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Side stories: optional pocket worlds, minutes long, own rules, own art | nothing | new — the whole **interlude** idea, at its most explicit |
| The genre itself alternates: sci-fi world, fantasy world | ten acts, ten pillars ([wave-design](wave-design.md)) | convergent; the pillar is this game's version |
| One player becomes an object the other operates | nothing | new — see **THE LATHE** |
| Two views of one space, one top-down and one from the side | the radar split ([systems](systems.md)) | new at this scale — see **THE EDGE** |
| Ride sections: a dragon, a bike, a board | nothing travels | refused |
| A 2D game running inside the 3D one | nothing | new — see **THE WELL** |

**The side stories are the clearest statement of the idea in either game.**
They are short, they are optional, they cost nothing to fail, they are built
out of one joke each, and they are thrown away. The pair enters, learns one
rule, does it four times, and leaves. Nothing carries over. That is a shape a
wave cannot be — a wave is authored against a pillar and belongs to an act —
and it is a shape this game could carry between acts at almost no cost to
anything already built.

## The finding

Both games escalate by **changing the verb**, and this one escalates by
changing what falls. Ten acts, ten pillars, one boss each: the pair gets better
at the same eleven columns for the whole run. That is a coherent design and it
is not what is being questioned here. What the reading says is that the design
has exactly one round shape, and both reference games have dozens, and the
distance between those two facts is where a run gets long enough to be tiring.

The answer is **not** more control schemes in the main game. It is a second
round shape that sits beside the field and borrows nothing from it: the
**interlude**. [interludes](interludes.md) is the frame — what one is, where it
sits, what failing costs, and what it may not do. Twelve of them are in
[the idea store](ideas.md#interludes).

**THE MIRROR is the proof this works, and nobody called it that.** The boss at
[bosses 11.3](bosses.md#113-the-mirror--your-own-ship-asking-for-your-moves-back)
suspends the field, plays Simon Says on the pair's own controls, and hands the
field back. It is an interlude wearing a boss's slot. It is also the argument
for the category being named: the next one of those, and there is one being
built, has nowhere to be filed.

## Shapes

Neither game is neon on black, and neither has anything to teach the bestiary.
What they do have is a rule about **contrast between rounds**: the side story
looks nothing like the level it hangs off, and that is most of why the break
reads as a break.

The catalogue already holds the vocabulary for that, and it is the half the
game does not currently spend. `slab` is a superellipse — "made rather than
grown" ([asset catalogue](../asset-catalogue.md)) — and `glyphed` is a rim of
travelling notches. **Every interlude below is drawn out of slabs and glyphs
and nothing else.** The field is blobs; the interlude is machinery; the pair
knows which round they are in before reading a word. That is one rule, it costs
no new art, and it does the work a loading screen would otherwise do.

## Animations

| Wanted | The nearest thing that exists | Note |
|---|---|---|

| Machinery moving in steps, not sways | `glyphed`'s travelling notches | own-motion is a wobble; an interlude moves on the beat or not at all |
| A needle, a dial, a gauge settling | nothing | the one genuinely missing motion — see **THE GAUGE** |

## Promoted to the idea store

Twelve, in [ideas.md](ideas.md#interludes), under a new group. Nothing was
promoted as a creature, a boss or a coupling, and that is the same finding
[transfers](transfers.md) reached from the other direction: these games do not
put their pressure on the things that come at you. They put it on what the two
of you are holding this minute, and on how often that changes.

## Refused, with the reason

- **Competitive minigames** — the valve is real and the answer is wrong here.
  Two devices, no shared screen, and the only channel that could settle a score
  is the one the whole game is made of. **The Fork** is the pause without the
  contest.
- **Dynamic split-screen** — there is no shared screen to split. What it bought
  is already recovered, deliberately narrowed, as **The Other Hand**
  ([transfers](transfers.md#the-other-hand)).
- **Ride sections** — a dragon, a bike, a board. Nothing the pair controls
  travels, and an interlude does not get to suspend that rule just because the
  hull is off screen. This is the one place the two games' best set pieces stop
  at the door.
- **Verbs discarded per level, in the main game** — tempting and wrong. The
  eleven columns and the two colours are what a pair spends ten acts learning
  to talk about, and a design that replaces them every act would be teaching a
  vocabulary it never lets anybody use. The interlude is where novelty goes
  precisely so that the field can stay still.
- **A story that explains the change of round** — both games motivate every
  swap with fiction, at length, in cutscenes. This one has a menu and then the
  field ([`?play` skips it](../../CLAUDE.md)), and no cutscene between the two:
  the world explains itself by being played ([briefings](briefings.md)). An
  interlude arrives and is understood, or it is the wrong interlude.
